Dear Hiring Manager / Selection Committee,

I'm applying for the Director of Enterprise Applications role because it formalizes work I'm already doing — and because the portfolio is at an inflection point that rewards the kind of leadership I've spent thirty years building toward. My title has been Principal UX Designer. My operating level has been somewhere else entirely.

Across our 65+ Django applications, I've watched us build UI-first and silo-by-silo — solving the same identity, provisioning, and personnel problems over and over because no shared capability layer exists to solve them once. My work over the past period has been about making that cost visible and building the alternative. Remora, the framework-agnostic component library I built on Lit, attacks our front-end fragmentation at the platform level instead of one template at a time. Otis, the personnel system I'm leading, is where I'm untangling the payroll and PeopleSoft integration successes & failures that have quietly eroded user trust into shadow spreadsheets. And when I mapped the new-hire onboarding pipeline — OAM to ServiceNow to AD to Otis to PeopleSoft — I surfaced a systemic cross-system failure with significant manual re-entry, PII traveling over email, and no shared identity key until provisioning. None of that is a UI problem. It's an architecture and operations problem, and it's the work this role exists to lead.

The thesis I keep returning to is simple: the organization that serves the Senate best over the next decade won't be the one with the most applications — it'll be the one with the fewest, most composable capabilities. That's why I've been pushing for an API-first operating model and a capability registry, using AI-agent readiness as the forcing function. We can't expose anything to an agent that we haven't first defined as a clean, governed capability. I'd rather we get ahead of that deliberately than have it imposed on us by a vendor demo that skipped architectural review.

This is a people role as much as a technical one, and that's where I've been investing. I've mentored a product owner from delivery-taker to genuine outcome-owner, partnered closely with our solutions architect, and consistently challenged project-model thinking in favor of durable product capability — constructively, with concrete examples, not abstractions. Earlier in my career I built design systems at MMIT that drove a 30% engineering velocity gain, which is the same lever I'd pull here: give developers reusable, well-governed building blocks and you get speed, consistency, and morale at once.

The track record outside these walls backs it up. At Reed Tech I halted a $1.2M rebuild by running a one-month discovery sprint that proved leadership was solving the wrong problem. At MMIT I identified a $5–10M API-first business-model opportunity that reshaped go-to-market. I'm SVPG-trained under Lea Hickman and certified through Product School. And inside SAA, I've already delivered measurable outcomes in this exact environment — cutting printed financial disclosures from 45% to under 20%.

I know this portfolio, this organization, and these constraints. I'm already cleared, already fluent in how we work, and already doing director-level discovery, architecture, and strategy as an individual contributor. The capability layer SAA needs doesn't fully exist yet — but the people closest to building it right are already here. I'd like the mandate to lead that work.

Thank you for your consideration.

Sincerely,
Brian Valachovic

Diagnosis & Ideas

Building Trust, Reducing Fear, & Celebrating the Right Things

In almost 3.5 years, I've been doing the tactical project work I'm assigned to. At the same time, I've been applying what I've learned in UX & Product Design when I'm assigned an output without evidence. Discovery. All solutions are a hypothesis when they're not accommpanied with context, evidence, and a clear outcome. This may look like defiance or resistance to some, and it's frequently resulted in the comment "we build what customers ask for", but I realize this reaction is caused by the fear that decades of decisions were made without evidence. But, it's table stakes for product management and design. I don't know how to operate any other way.

Teams today build what they are asked to build. Worse, they provide the estimates they believe leadership wants to hear. This is because the team has prided itself on speed. But, speed has been an illusion. Stakeholders waiting 3 years for funding is not speed. It's process failure. A team responsible for a portfolio of this size, that operates the same way it did when it had 10 applications is a team that has not matured because the status quo could not be challenged. Instead, they looked to ways to build faster, reduce tech and UX debt faster - OUTPUT faster. All non-starters.

  • 3-5 month cycles where Agile makes any scope possible is fantasy
  • Software & knowledge degrades the moment the team departs
  • Our "apps" are accidental platforms
  • Product architecture, data modeling & governance is seen as "over-engineering" and a barrier to speed
  • Technical & UX debt is a HUGE tax for skipping architecture & design
  • This has created a culture of short-term thinking and long-term debt
  • KTLO work is hidden
  • Unbalanced budgets: 100% of work is new
  • Rewrites/modernization efforts are HUGE RED FLAGS.
  • This has created false expectations for leadership
  • Project funding only exacerbates the problem
  • Focusing on outcomes has created a false understanding of the product operating model - there's much more to it
  • Project model = no ownership, a prerequisite for product management principles
The Car Manufacturer Analogy
            Close your eyes. Imagine a pristine car manufacturing plant.
            At the center of the plant is the Engine Team - the specialists who build the safest, most secure
            engines the company has ever produced. This year, they’ve built a new engine: Okta. Leadership
            has mandated that all vehicles must be upgraded to this new engine by the fall.
            The Engine Team is proud. The new engine is modern, secure, and powerful.
            But there’s a problem.
            The new engine arrives missing a critical component:
            authorization.
            Now imagine the Car Teams.
            These teams build the vehicles themselves. They are excellent at what they do - seasoned
            engineers who know how to fix tires, adjust steering columns, tune suspensions, and solve
            every problem outside the engine compartment.
            Historically, they’ve never needed to worry about engines.
            The old engine always worked.
            It always handled identity.
            It always handled authorization.
            It always made the car run.
            But now the old engine is going away.
            And the new engine - Okta - removes the very system the cars depended on.
            Copilot may make mistakes 2
            The Car Teams react the only way they know how.
            They start rewiring dashboards.
            They resize wheels.
            They move steering columns.
            They invent “personas.”
            They create custom access logic.
            They patch and glue and retrofit.
            They’re solving tire problems.
            They’re solving steering problems.
            They’re solving dashboard problems.
            But they never pop the hood.
            They never say to leadership:
            “This new engine is missing its authorization system.
            We aren’t engine builders.
            IAM needs to deliver this.”
            Not because they’re incapable - but because the culture has taught them that the only
            acceptable answer is motion.
            Get the car rolling.
            Show progress.
            Don’t say “stop.”
            Don’t say “this isn’t our domain.”
            Don’t say “the engine team needs to fix this.”
            So the Car Teams do what they’ve always done:
            They push the car downhill so leadership sees movement.
            The result?
            Cars that move only when pushed by many people.
            Cars that are slow, fragile, and resource-dependent.
            Cars that require constant firefighting.
            Cars that could be flying down the road - if the engine had been complete.
            This isn’t a failure of the Car Teams.
            It’s a failure of the system.
            The Engine Team delivered authentication.
            But the engine is missing authorization.
            And the Car Teams are terrified to say:
            “This isn’t our expertise.”
            In healthy product organizations, that moment - the moment someone says “this isn’t our
            domain” - is celebrated. Because leadership understands the cost of firefighting. They know that
            pushing a car downhill is not the same as building a car that drives itself.
            Copilot may make mistakes 3
            The opportunity ahead
            The real modernization work is not in rewiring dashboards or resizing wheels.
            It’s in delivering the missing engine component - authorization - so the Car Teams can stop
            pushing cars downhill and start building vehicles that actually drive.
            And that requires one thing:
            Someone willing to pop the hood.

Questions for Ray Woeller

Director-level questions for a CTO phone screen

Mandate & Scope

  • As you think about the future of EAD, and maybe more importantly, technology at the SAA, how important is it to you that we have a shared, evidence-based understanding of the portfolio, so that strategy and prioritization are based in objectivity, and can be addressed intentionally, not reactively? How does this role fit into that vision?
  • What does success look like for this role in the first 6 months, and how would you distinguish that from success at 18–24 months?
  • Is this role being created to formalize work that's already happening informally, or is it meant to reset direction? What's the honest gap between the job description and where the org actually is today?
  • How much authority would this role have over prioritization across the application portfolio versus advising into decisions made elsewhere?

Capability Governance & API-First Strategy

  • Has there been prior appetite — or prior failed attempts — at a shared capability layer or API-first model across the portfolio? What happened last time?
  • Who owns architectural review today when a new system or integration gets proposed, and how enforceable is that review in practice?
  • As AI-agent readiness becomes a forcing function for clean, governed capabilities, how is your office thinking about sequencing that work against existing modernization priorities?

Organizational Priorities

  • Of the ~65+ applications in the portfolio, which ones represent the highest institutional risk right now — technical debt, vendor risk, or single points of failure?
  • Where does leadership feel the most pain today: fragmentation, delivery speed, security/compliance, or trust in data across systems?
  • How aligned are the CIO's office and the business units on what "enterprise" application development should mean going forward?

Decision Authority & Structure

  • Who would this role report to day-to-day, and who are the peer directors or architects this role needs to build alignment with?
  • What budget or staffing authority comes with this role versus what needs to be requested project by project?
  • How are competing priorities between individual application teams and enterprise-wide capability work typically resolved today?

Success Metrics

  • What metrics does your office currently track for the health of the application portfolio, and which of those do you trust versus which feel more vanity than signal?
  • If I'm effective in this role a year from now, what will have measurably changed — fewer duplicate systems, faster delivery, fewer manual/PII-handling workarounds, something else?
