## Core Beliefs

Most product failures aren’t build failures. They’re problem-definition failures. I’ve spent 30 years learning to find the real problem, test the riskiest assumption cheaply, and teach teams to keep doing it without me. These beliefs weren’t written in theory. They’re survival tools from working in organizations that rewarded shipping over learning, and they became principles.

### Every Solution Is a Bet

Every solution rests on assumptions, and some of them are large and risky. Most ideas that arrive with confidence are bets, and some are worth making. Others turn out to be a million dollar lesson. The only way to know which is to test.

AI has made building cheap, which makes it tempting to skip the question of what’s worth building. The result is more output and more noise, and the experience gets worse for everyone. The discipline of finding the right problem matters more now, not less.

That’s why I don’t stop at discovery. I find the riskiest assumption and build the fastest test of it, usually a working prototype in front of real users, before an organization commits a team to it.

### Bring a Problem, Not a Solution

Teams do their best work when leadership brings a problem and context instead of a solution. When a team underperforms, the first question I ask isn’t “what’s wrong with the team?” It’s “what did they have to work with?”

Here’s what makes the difference. Leadership’s job is to come to the team with:

- A problem or opportunity, not a solution
- Strategic context: how this aligns with organizational goals, and where the guardrails are
- The “who”: who is experiencing this problem and who benefits from solving it
- The cost of inaction: what happens if we don’t act
- A definition of success: what “solved” actually looks like
- A time horizon: how much investment is worth making before we know if this is working

When leadership brings all of this, something remarkable happens. The team comes back with creativity, technical ingenuity, and options: in one sprint we can validate this, in two we can build it, in a quarter we can ship it. They take calculated risks and make bets. That’s where the real value gets created.

A solution, on the other hand, signals that nobody trusts the team to find the right answer, whether leadership intends that or not. The conversation shifts from outcomes to estimates, engineers spend days calculating how long the solution will take, and nobody asks whether it should be built at all. This isn’t a failure of intent. Most organizations reward certainty and delivery, so handing down a solution is the rational move. It’s also the expensive one.

### The Formula 1 Problem

I’ve seen this scenario more times than I can count. Leadership arrives with a mandate: build us a Formula 1 car. The team executes. Twelve to eighteen months later it’s delivered, exactly to spec and beautifully engineered. Customers ignore it. Sales can’t move it. Not a single contract gets signed.

Nobody stopped to ask what problem the car was solving. When someone finally does, the answer is often a blank stare, and that stare is one of the most expensive expressions in business. It means the organization is about to test an assumption with a production budget.

Given the right context, the team doesn’t build a Formula 1 car. They ask who needs to go where, when, and why. They learn it’s a suburban customer getting groceries delivered between 6:30 and 7:00 PM every other Friday, who also needs a ride afterward, and that the organization is focused on short-trip pickup and delivery. So they build a Honda CRV. It costs less, ships faster, fits the lane perfectly, and customers love it.

That’s not a lesser outcome. That’s product thinking working as it should. The leader’s job is to provide context and then get comfortable with uncertainty about what the team will build. In my experience, that uncertainty resolves into something better than what leadership imagined, and faster than anyone expected.

### Evidence Over Assumptions

Most product failures don’t happen because a team built the wrong solution. They happen because nobody questioned whether they were solving the right problem. I’ve saved over $1.2 million by stopping projects that had no evidence behind them. Not because I’m smarter than anyone else, but because I insisted on testing assumptions before committing resources.

Evidence-based product development means:

- Discovery before delivery, always
- Small bets to validate big ideas
- Research that asks “should we?” and not just “how should we?”
- Being willing to hear “this is a bad idea” and act on it

The hardest part isn’t finding evidence. It’s building a culture where evidence is allowed to change a decision people are attached to. Evidence gives a team permission to ask “but should we?”, and the courage to use it.

### Outcomes Over Outputs

Feature factories are comfortable. You know what you’re building, the roadmap looks impressive, stakeholders get their requests, and everyone feels productive. But you’re probably building the wrong things.

Outcome-focused work is less comfortable. It means:

- Saying no to features that don’t move the needle
- Measuring what matters, not what’s easy
- Focusing deeply on problems, not solutions
- Accepting that “we shipped nothing” might be the right answer

I drove a personnel system from paper workflows to 100% electronic submission by rejecting competing features and focusing on complete payroll integration. We could have shipped a dozen small features that made stakeholders happy. Instead, we shipped one complete solution that eliminated 400+ hours of annual manual work.

Outputs are what you ship. Outcomes are what changes. Most organizations reward outputs because they’re visible and predictable. Outcomes take patience and discipline, and a willingness to look unproductive while you validate whether the work matters.

### Trust Makes the Rest Possible

None of this works without trust between the people building the product. Where trust is scarce, people hide mistakes, hoard information, and protect themselves instead of solving problems. I work to build environments where:

“I don’t know” is a strength, because we can learn together
People bring problems before they become crises
Failure is data, not a career-ending event
Disagreement is healthy, not dangerous

I’ve built trust-based teams inside difficult organizations. It’s possible, and it’s slow. It also gets easier when it’s modeled from the top, because it all starts with vulnerability.

If you’re sitting on a roadmap full of confident bets, or a backlog nobody remembers the reason for, I’d like to hear about it.
